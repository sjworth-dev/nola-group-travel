Yes, you can walk down the street with a drink in New Orleans. That's the short answer, and it's the reason half your group already showed up assuming they can carry a daiquiri anywhere in the city with no consequences. They can't. Here's the actual rule.

New Orleans allows open containers on public streets and sidewalks, but the law has real conditions: plastic or foam only, no glass, no cans, and it doesn't cover the whole city. Get this wrong with a group of fifteen and you're the reason someone's holding a citation on day one. Get it right and it's one less thing anyone has to think about for the rest of the trip.

This guide covers what the ordinance actually says, where it applies, where it quietly stops applying, and what that means for how you move a big group between bars, pools, and parade routes.

## Quick Planning Checklist

- [ ] Confirm your group knows the material rule: plastic/foam to-go cups only, never glass or cans
- [ ] Ask every bar for a to-go cup before you leave—most French Quarter and Frenchmen Street bars offer one automatically
- [ ] Don't assume the rule applies outside downtown and the Quarter—residential neighborhoods are a different story
- [ ] Plan your bar-crawl route around bars that pour to-go without a fight
- [ ] Keep pool-day drinks in plastic cups if you're walking between a rental and a nearby spot
- [ ] Designate someone to flag open containers before you board a rideshare or bus—drinking in most vehicles is a separate violation
- [ ] Know that "open container legal" doesn't mean "no rules apply"—public intoxication and disorderly conduct are still enforceable

## The Actual Rule

New Orleans' open container law permits alcohol in public in specific zones, under specific conditions. It is not a blanket "drink anywhere" law, and treating it like one is the single most common mistake group trips make.

**The container matters.** Plastic and foam cups are allowed. Glass bottles and aluminum cans are not permitted on the street—if you buy a beer in a can or a drink in a glass, you need to transfer it to a plastic to-go cup before you walk out. Most bars in the French Quarter and Frenchmen Street corridor hand these out without being asked. If a bartender doesn't offer one, ask. It's a normal request, not an imposition.

**Container size has a practical ceiling.** There's no need to carry anything larger than a standard cup. Oversized "yard" containers and novelty cups sold on Bourbon Street are marketed as souvenirs, not a loophole—treat them the same as any other to-go cup: no glass, no cans, walk don't drive.

**This is about walking, not driving.** Open container laws for pedestrians on public sidewalks are a completely different legal category from drinking in a moving vehicle. Louisiana law prohibits open containers of alcohol in the passenger area of a vehicle on a public road, full stop—driver or passenger. A group piling into a rideshare or a charter van with drinks in hand is a real risk, not a technicality—see the [transportation guide](/guides/transportation-guide/) for how groups typically move between spots. Finish the drink or leave it before you get in the vehicle.

## Where It Applies (and Where It Doesn't)

This is the part most first-time group organizers get wrong: the open container allowance is not citywide.

| Area | Open Container Status |
|------|----------------------|
| French Quarter | Allowed (plastic/foam cups) |
| Frenchmen Street / Marigny commercial strip | Allowed (plastic/foam cups) |
| CBD / Central Business District | Allowed (plastic/foam cups) |
| Official parade routes during Mardi Gras and other parades | Allowed along the route while the parade permit is active |
| Residential streets (Uptown, Bywater side streets, Garden District, most of Mid-City) | Not covered—treat as a standard public-drinking violation zone |
| Parks (unless part of a permitted festival footprint) | Generally not covered outside a festival's designated boundary |
| Inside vehicles, including rideshares and buses | Never allowed, regardless of location |

The practical takeaway: the tourist core—Quarter, CBD, Frenchmen—is where the "you can drink on the street" reputation actually holds up. The moment your group walks a few blocks into a residential pocket to get back to a rental, that allowance is gone. This matters for groups staying in villa-style accommodation in neighborhoods like the [Bywater](/neighborhoods/bywater/) or the [Lower Garden District](/neighborhoods/lower-garden-district/)—the walk from the bar to the front door may cross out of the zone where an open cup is fine.

Festival grounds (Jazz Fest, French Quarter Fest) operate under their own permitted footprint, which is a separate legal mechanism from the city's standing open container ordinance—inside the festival gates, different rules govern alcohol entirely, and outside vendor alcohol generally isn't permitted in. Don't assume festival rules and street rules are the same thing.

## How This Changes Group Logistics

A group of ten to twenty people moving as a pack changes the math on all of this. One person with a can in hand is a non-issue for enforcement. A visible group of fifteen walking down a residential street with open glass bottles is a different picture, and it's the kind of thing that draws attention fast.

**Bar crawls:** Route your [bar crawl](/guides/bar-crawl-guide/) through zones where the to-go cup handoff is routine—French Quarter and Frenchmen Street bars are built around this. If your route dips into a neighborhood stretch to reach the next stop, that's the moment to finish drinks rather than carry them.

**Pool days at a rental:** If your group is staying somewhere with a private pool and wandering to a nearby corner store or a neighbor's porch, remember the rule resets outside the tourist zones. A pool day at the house doesn't need to think about any of this—open container laws only govern public space, not your own rental property.

**Parade days:** During Mardi Gras, the parade route itself is treated as an active public event corridor where the open container allowance is honored more liberally, but the moment the parade passes and the crowd disperses into residential blocks, that liberality goes with it. The [Mardi Gras group guide](/guides/mardi-gras-group-guide/) covers viewing-spot logistics in more depth.

**Getting between spots at night:** This is where groups most often slip up. Walking from a bar to a rideshare pickup point with a drink still in hand, then trying to bring it into the car, is the single most common violation pattern for visiting groups. Finish it, toss it, or hand it off before the car door opens.

## What Actually Gets Enforced

New Orleans police are not aggressively hunting open container violations in the French Quarter—the entire tourism economy assumes people are walking around with drinks. What actually draws attention:

1. **Glass or cans in hand**, which is the clearest, most visible violation of the material rule
2. **Visible public intoxication**—stumbling, disorderly behavior, or someone who can't stand unassisted is a separate and more serious issue than the cup itself
3. **Drinking in a parked or moving vehicle**, which is enforced more strictly than street drinking
4. **Large glass bottles being passed around a group**, which reads as a bigger problem than one person's cup

A group that's loud but orderly, carrying plastic cups, isn't the target of enforcement. A group that's visibly overserved, carrying glass, or causing a disturbance is a different story regardless of the container question.

## Pro Tips

1. **Ask for a to-go cup before you leave every bar.** It's standard practice, not an unusual request. Bartenders in the Quarter and on Frenchmen Street do this dozens of times a night.

2. **Assign one person per subgroup to watch containers before rideshare pickup.** It takes ten seconds and avoids the most common violation in the whole list.

3. **Don't bring drinks into a charter bus, van, or rideshare.** Open containers in a vehicle are treated as a real violation, not a gray area, no matter how relaxed the street rule feels.

4. **Treat residential neighborhoods differently than the tourist core.** If you're walking back to a rental in a quieter part of town, that's not the zone the "you can drink here" reputation is describing.

5. **Never carry glass bottles or aluminum cans on the street**, even briefly. If a bar serves your drink in glass and you want to walk with it, ask them to pour it into plastic first.

6. **Understand that open container legal doesn't mean consequence-free.** Public intoxication and disorderly conduct are separately enforceable and matter more to how your night goes than the cup material.

7. **For festival grounds, follow the festival's own alcohol policy**, not the city's standing street rule—they're different systems even though both involve public drinking.

## Large Groups and the Open Container Rule

A big group changes the visibility math on all of this. Fifteen people walking down Bourbon Street with plastic cups blend in completely. Fifteen people doing the same thing on a quiet residential block outside the tourist zones stand out, and that's usually where a group unfamiliar with the actual boundaries of the law gets a surprise.

This is one more reason where you stay matters. A rental in a walkable, tourist-adjacent zone means your group's natural drink-in-hand movement patterns stay inside the areas where that's actually permitted. A rental buried deep in a residential neighborhood means more of your walking happens in a gray zone, and more reminders are needed about finishing a drink before you leave the bar. Property options built for large groups—villas in the Bywater, the Lower Garden District, and elsewhere—vary a lot on exactly this kind of walkability, which is worth weighing alongside bedroom count and pool size.
