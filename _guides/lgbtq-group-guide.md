Verdict: New Orleans is one of the easiest cities in the country to bring a big queer group to. Start the night at Bourbon and St. Ann, finish it in the Marigny, and decide early whether you want Southern Decadence or a weekend when the Quarter is merely busy.

The welcome here is old, not recent. Carnival built a whole civic culture around costume, performance, and ignoring what the neighbors think, and queer New Orleans grew up inside it. The gay bars on lower Bourbon were pouring decades before Stonewall.

The practical upshot: twenty people walking to a bar together draws no more attention than any other twenty people walking to a bar. Which, in the French Quarter, is none.

Still picking dates and a headcount? [Start with the planning hub](/guides/plan-a-group-trip/), then come back.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Decide whether the trip is built around an event (Southern Decadence, Pride, Carnival, Easter) or deliberately avoids one
- [ ] Southern Decadence 2027 runs September 2–6 — if that's your weekend, sort the house first
- [ ] Check the official New Orleans Pride site for this year's dates (typically June)
- [ ] Orient at Bourbon and St. Ann — the gay stretch of the Quarter runs downriver from that corner
- [ ] Budget one night for the Marigny and Bywater instead of Bourbon
- [ ] Reserve group dinners early — the kitchens are welcoming, the big tables are finite

- [ ] Book accommodations early — for the largest selection, book 6+ months out

## Why New Orleans Specifically

Most Southern cities have a gay scene. New Orleans has a gay history, and you can still drink in it.

**The bars are old.** Café Lafitte in Exile on Bourbon has been open since the 1930s and bills itself as the oldest continuously operating gay bar in the country. Nobody opened it to capture a demographic.

**Carnival has gay krewes.** Krewes such as Petronius, Amon-Ra, and Armeinius have staged elaborate Carnival balls for decades. They are mostly ball krewes rather than street-parade krewes, so the public face of gay Carnival is the costume scene in the Quarter on Fat Tuesday.

**The neighborhoods match the bars.** The Marigny and Bywater have long had large queer residential populations. You are not visiting a district; you are having coffee where people live.

**The history has weight, too.** A sidewalk plaque at Chartres and Iberville marks the UpStairs Lounge, where a 1973 arson killed 32 people. It takes five minutes to visit and it reframes the party down the street.

## The Bourbon Street Gay Scene

Walk down Bourbon away from Canal until you hit St. Ann Street. The crowd changes at that corner, and the next couple of blocks, plus a few side streets, are the center of French Quarter gay nightlife.

| Venue | Character | Group notes |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| Oz | Dance club at Bourbon and St. Ann, balcony upstairs | The room with the most space for a full crew |
| Bourbon Pub & Parade | Video bar downstairs, dance floor and balcony upstairs, across from Oz | Easy to trade back and forth with Oz all night |
| Café Lafitte in Exile | Historic bar a block further down, balcony, famously late hours | Go in shifts — it's a bar, not a venue |
| Good Friends | Neighborhood bar a block off Bourbon on Dauphine | The quieter round when the group needs to hear each other |

One naming trap: Café Lafitte in Exile is the gay bar. Lafitte's Blacksmith Shop, a block away, is the candlelit old tavern everyone photographs. Both are worth a drink. Only one is what your group chat means.

**With the whole crew:** Oz and the Pub can absorb a big group. Everything else on this stretch is smaller, so split up and regroup at the corner. Walk-around drinks mean nobody is anchored to one tab.

**Honest assessment:** This is still Bourbon Street. It is loud, touristy, and sticky, and for a lot of groups that is exactly the assignment. If yours wants something more local, give Bourbon one night and spend the rest downriver. ## Beyond Bourbon Street

### Frenchmen Street and the Marigny

The Marigny sits just across Esplanade from the Quarter. Frenchmen Street's music clubs are welcoming without being labeled, and the neighborhood has its own gay bars a few blocks further in — The Phoenix is the long-running leather-and-Levi's bar, and the AllWays Lounge on St. Claude runs cabaret, drag, and burlesque.

**Works for groups that want:** live music, a mixed room, and fewer bachelor parties per square foot than Bourbon.

For shows specifically, the [drag show guide](/guides/nola-group-drag-show-guide/) covers how to book a table for a big party.

### Bywater

One neighborhood further downriver. Art-forward, residential, smaller bar scene, more locals.

**Bacchanal Wine** is the obvious group move: a wine shop with a backyard and live music. Go in the late afternoon, before the bar night. Check current hours and whether they'll take a large party before you promise it to anyone.

### Magazine Street

The Uptown shopping-and-restaurant corridor is not queer-focused, just comfortable. Good for a daytime wander and a group dinner where the point is the food.

| Event | When | Scale | Best for |
|-------|------|-------|----------|

| New Orleans Pride | Typically June — confirm on the official site | Mid-size, local | Community over spectacle |
| Gay Easter Parade | Easter Sunday (March 28, 2027) | Small, charming | A spring trip with one built-in event |
| Fat Tuesday in the Quarter | February 9, 2027 | Citywide | Costume people |

### Southern Decadence

Labor Day weekend. This is the big one.

Decadence fills the French Quarter with crowds well into six figures across five days, with the walking parade on Sunday afternoon. The street scene is free. The pool parties and club nights are ticketed.

Two things to know before you commit. It is hot — early September is still deep summer here. And it sits in the statistical peak of hurricane season, so book flights you can change and read the cancellation terms on the house.

If you're going, build the whole trip around it. The [Southern Decadence guide](/guides/lgbtq-southern-decadence-guide/) covers the parade, the party tiers, and how to structure the days.

### New Orleans Pride

Pride here is smaller and more local than Decadence, with a parade and events around the Quarter and Marigny. June is soft season: hot, humid, and noticeably cheaper than spring. Dates are set by organizers each year, so confirm before booking flights.

### Easter and Carnival

The Gay Easter Parade rolls through the Quarter on Easter Sunday — carriages, hats, a good excuse for brunch. It's a pleasant add-on to a spring trip rather than a reason to book one.

Carnival is the reverse: a reason to book, with gay Carnival as one layer of it. On Fat Tuesday the Quarter's costume scene peaks around the long-running Bourbon Street Awards costume contest (its location has moved over the years — check the current one). The [Mardi Gras group guide](/guides/mardi-gras-group-guide/) handles parade logistics, and the [best time to visit guide](/guides/nola-group-best-time-to-visit-guide/) shows where each of these weekends sits in the year.

## Dining

Short version: eat wherever you were going to eat. The city's restaurant industry has plenty of queer owners, chefs, and staff, and a big table gets the same treatment regardless of who's at it.

The real constraint is size, not welcome. Twenty people need a private room or a set menu almost anywhere good. The [restaurant guide](/guides/restaurant-guide/) covers who can actually seat you.

## Getting Around

- **Quarter to Marigny:** walk it. Frenchmen is a few minutes past the end of Bourbon.
- **Bywater:** a short rideshare, or a longer walk in daylight.
- **Uptown and the Lower Garden District:** the St. Charles streetcar gets you to Canal Street; rideshare home late.
- **Late night with a full group:** order several cars at once from a corner off Bourbon, not on it.

## Pro Tips

1. **Treat St. Ann as the meeting point.** "Corner of Bourbon and St. Ann" is the one instruction everyone in the group can follow at 1 AM.

2. **Do both scenes.** Bourbon is historic and festive. The Marigny is where the night gets interesting. Choosing one is a mistake.

3. **Pace for a city without a mandated last call.** Plenty of bars run very late and a few never close. Start later than you think, and eat a real dinner.

4. **Bring the costumes.** Coordinated looks are a civic virtue here year-round. Decadence, Halloween, and Fat Tuesday reward effort; nobody will think you overdid it.

5. **Pick your heat tolerance honestly.** Pride (June) and Decadence (Labor Day) both land in hot months. If the group wants patio weather, come in spring or October and pay peak-season prices for it.

6. **Give the daytime a plan.** Bar nights fill themselves. The afternoons are where a big group drifts. A pool, a long lunch, or Bacchanal solves it.

7. **Look past the bars if community matters.** The city has active LGBTQ+ organizations and a history worth an hour of your trip. The UpStairs Lounge plaque is a start.

## Where a Big Group Sleeps

This is the hard part of any double-digit trip, and it gets harder on event weekends.

A whole house does one thing a hotel can't: it gives the crew a shared space with no audience. The pre-party, the pool afternoon, and the 3 AM debrief all happen at home. A room block does the opposite thing well: separate keys, separate bills, and nobody negotiating over the primary bedroom.

Some fits, by geography:

- **Close to the Marigny and Frenchmen:** Bywater has several large-group houses. The Mazant, an 1880s guesthouse that sleeps 18 with a heated pool, is one example; The Lookout Inn suits a crew of about a dozen.
- **Choice and short-notice availability:** Heirloom has the deepest inventory of large-format homes, mostly in the Lower Garden District and Central City.
- **Mixed budgets, own keys:** a hotel room block. The [hotel vs. villa guide](/guides/hotel-vs-villa-guide/) runs the math.
