New Orleans took Pamplona's Running of the Bulls, swapped the bulls for roller-derby skaters swinging plastic wiffle bats, and turned it into a citywide costume party. That's San Fermín in Nola, and it is exactly the kind of gloriously absurd thing this city does better than anywhere else.

Every July, thousands of people dress in white with red sashes and bandanas, gather in the CBD/Warehouse District before the heat gets murderous, and get "chased" through the streets by the Big Easy Rollergirls and other derby leagues wielding bats instead of horns. Nobody gets gored. Everybody gets a welt or two and a great story. Then the whole thing pours into block parties that bracket the run on both ends.

For a big group, this is one of the best single-morning anchors on the summer calendar — cheap, chaotic, communal, and over before the worst of the afternoon heat. The catch is that it's a July morning event in New Orleans, which means the planning is less about the run and more about surviving the day around it.

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Confirm the exact date, start time, and route on the official San Fermín in Nola site before booking anything — it's mid-July, but the specific weekend and staging point move year to year
- [ ] Get everyone into the dress code early: all white, red sash (faja) at the waist, red bandana (pañuelo) at the neck
- [ ] Decide who's running and who's spectating — not everyone in a twenty-person crew wants to get whacked with a bat at 8am
- [ ] Buy in advance if the run requires registration or wristbands; the closing party is often ticketed separately
- [ ] Plan the morning around an early start — this is a beat-the-heat event, and the run goes off early for a reason
- [ ] Hydrate the night before, not just the morning of; a July run on a hangover is a medical event
- [ ] Set a rally point and a plan for after the run — the block parties are where the group reconvenes

## What San Fermín in Nola Actually Is

Strip away the costume and it's three things stacked together over one weekend.

**The opening party (El Txupinazo).** Pamplona kicks off its festival with an opening ceremony, and New Orleans borrows the name. The night before the run, there's a launch party — costumes, music, sangria, the official countdown to the run. This is where the weekend's energy sets.

**The run itself.** The morning event. Runners in white and red gather at the staging point, the bulls — derby skaters with bats — are released behind them, and everyone runs the route through the CBD/Warehouse District streets. The "bulls" catch the slow, the bold, and the ones who linger to pose. Getting swatted is the point, not the failure.

**The closing party (La Fiesta de los Muertos).** After the run, the crowd converges on a block party to recover, rehydrate, compare bruises, and keep the costume going. This is the long tail of the day and often where a group spends the most time.

A crew can do all three or just the run. Knowing which pieces your group actually wants shapes the whole plan. For where this sits against the rest of the year's events, the [festival season calendar](/guides/festival-season-calendar/) puts it in context.

> Don't state the specific date, start time, staging point, or route from memory — they change year to year. Confirm everything on the official San Fermín in Nola site. This guide is about how to plan, not what's on this year's map.

## The Dress Code Is Not Optional

The uniform is the whole aesthetic, and it's genuinely easy to nail:

- **White top and bottom.** Whatever you've got — a white tee and white shorts is fine. This is a group of thousands in white; showing up in street clothes makes you the odd one out and, in July, the hotter one out.
- **Red faja (sash).** A red sash tied at the waist. A length of red fabric works.
- **Red pañuelo (bandana).** A red bandana or scarf at the neck. This is the piece people photograph.

Two practical notes. First, white plus a plastic bat means you will get red marks and dirt on the outfit — wear white you don't care about. Second, white is the smartest possible color for a July morning in New Orleans; the tradition doubles as heat management. The [outdoor festival heat management guide](/guides/nola-group-outdoor-festival-heat-management/) covers the rest of the July survival kit.

For a big group, buy the sashes and bandanas in bulk before the trip. Twenty people improvising red fabric the morning of is chaos; one person ordering a pack of red bandanas is a five-minute fix that also makes your crew findable in the crowd.

## Runner or Spectator: Split the Group Honestly

Not everyone wants to be chased and swatted through the streets at breakfast. That's fine. The event works for both, and forcing the whole crew into the run is how you lose the people who'd rather watch.

| Group member | Run or watch? | Why |
|--------------|---------------|-----|
| The thrill-seekers | Run | The bruise is a badge; this is the whole reason they came |
| The costume-committed | Run or watch | In it for the aesthetic and the party more than the chase |
| The heat-averse | Watch | Spectating from shade beats sprinting in July |
| The hungover | Watch (or sit it out) | A July run on no sleep is a bad idea; the block party is the reward either way |
| Anyone with mobility or health concerns | Watch | The run is crowded, fast, and unpredictable underfoot |

The move for a mixed crew: runners stage together, spectators pick a shaded, elevated spot along the route with sightlines, and everyone reconvenes at the block party after. Nobody's dragged into something they didn't want, and the group still shares the day.

## The Season Reality: A July Morning in New Orleans

Here's the part out-of-town groups underestimate. July is New Orleans' soft season — lower rates, thinner tourist crowds, and brutal heat, humidity, and near-daily afternoon thunderstorms. Running of the Bulls is a demand blip inside that soft month, not a Mardi Gras-scale citywide takeover, so lodging is generally easier to come by than it is in the spring high season.

What that means for the day:

- **The run goes off early on purpose.** Morning is the only humane window for physical activity outdoors in July. Respect the start time; there's no "we'll catch the noon run."
- **The heat is the real opponent.** By late morning it's oppressive. The event is built to be over before the worst of it — don't extend the outdoor portion past what your group can handle.
- **Afternoon rain is likely.** Build the back half of the day indoors and air-conditioned. The block party is the transition; after that, plan for shelter.

If your group is coming specifically for this, pair it with the broader [summer trip guide](/guides/nola-group-summer-trip-guide/) — the run is a great anchor, but a July trip needs a heat-aware plan around it.

## Where This Happens, and Getting There

The run stages and routes through the CBD and Warehouse/Arts District — flat, walkable, central streets that make it easy to keep a big crew together before the start and to peel spectators off to good vantage points. That central footprint is also why it's a low-logistics event by New Orleans festival standards: no fairgrounds, no shuttle, no quarter-mile between your group and the action.

A few movement notes for a group:

- **Don't drive to it.** Street closures and a crowd in white make parking a nightmare. Rideshare, walk, or take the streetcar to the edge of the district and walk in.
- **Stage as a group before the run, not during.** Once the bulls are loose, you will not be herding twenty people. Meet early, take the group photo, then let runners and spectators split.
- **Know your after-run rally point cold.** Phones die, crowds swallow people, and everyone's dressed identically. A named corner and a time beats "text me."

When the group is ready for a real meal, the Warehouse District has the density to seat a crew — the [Warehouse District dining guide](/guides/nola-group-warehouse-district-dining-guide/) covers where a big group can actually land a table nearby. ## A Loose Day-Shape for a Group

Not a rigid schedule — a rhythm that survives a July morning.

| Time | Move |
|------|------|
| Night before | Opening party (optional), lay out costumes, hydrate, early-ish night |
| Early morning | Coffee, light breakfast, sunscreen, get into white-and-red at the base |
| Pre-run | Rideshare/walk to the staging area, group photo, split runners from spectators |
| The run | Runners run; spectators watch from shade with sightlines |
| Late morning | Reconvene at the closing block party — rehydrate, recover, keep the costume going |
| Early afternoon | AC break at the base; the heat and likely rain make this non-negotiable |
| Evening | Reconvene for dinner or a bar with everyone's stories fully formed |

The point is a shape that lets people opt in and out of the physical part without fracturing the group.

## Pro Tips

1. **Order the red gear in bulk before you fly in.** A pack of red bandanas and sashes for the whole crew is cheaper, faster, and more coordinated than twenty people scrambling the morning of. It also makes your group instantly findable in a sea of white.

2. **Sunscreen and hydrate the night before.** July in New Orleans punishes the unprepared. The single biggest group-longevity move is water before the run, not just during.

3. **Wear white you can sacrifice.** Plastic bats, street dirt, and spilled sangria all end up on the outfit. This is a feature — a clean white outfit at the end means you didn't commit.

4. **Closed-toe shoes for runners.** It's a fast, crowded run on city streets. Sandals are how you leave with a real injury instead of a fun welt. Save the cute shoes for the block party.

5. **Assign a photographer who isn't running.** The best shots are of the run, and you can't take them while getting chased. One designated spectator with a phone gets the group the images it actually wants.

6. **The block party is the event, too.** Don't treat the run as the whole thing and bail after. The closing party is where the group reconvenes, the costumes peak, and the day's stories get told. Budget time for it.

7. **Have a hard indoor plan for the afternoon.** Post-run, the heat and the likely thunderstorm will end the outdoor portion of your day whether you planned for it or not. A booked lunch, a museum, or the base with AC keeps the group from melting into a scattered afternoon.

## For a Big Group Specifically

Running of the Bulls is easier on large-group logistics than most of the city's festivals — it's short, central, flat, and cheap, with no fairgrounds or ticketed gate to funnel twenty people through. The strain isn't the run; it's the July day around it. A crew scattered across separate hotel rooms with no shared base tends to fracture on a hot morning with an early start — people oversleep, miss the staging, and never fully regroup.

A single large-format base fixes most of that. One address to stage from before the run, a pool and AC for the mandatory afternoon recovery, and communal space for everyone to get into costume and reconvene after.
