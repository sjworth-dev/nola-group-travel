Every big New Orleans festival wants you standing in a crowd with a drink in hand. The Tennessee Williams & New Orleans Literary Festival wants you sitting in a panel discussion, then standing in Jackson Square screaming "STELLA!" at the top of your lungs. It's a genuinely different kind of festival week, and it's the rare one built for a group that includes at least one person who'd rather talk about a play than dance to a brass band.

The festival runs late March, which lands it inside the city's spring high season alongside Mardi Gras, [French Quarter Fest](/guides/french-quarter-fest/), and Jazz Fest — so don't mistake "lower-key" for "off-season." What makes it different is the format: panels and master classes with writers, staged readings, literary walking tours through the French Quarter, and the festival's signature public event, the Stanley and Stella Shouting Contest, where contestants reenact Stanley Kowalski's famous cry from *A Streetcar Named Desire* in front of a crowd in Jackson Square.

For a group that wants a New Orleans trip built around something other than a bar crawl or a parade route, this is the anchor. Here's how it works and how to run it with a crew.

---

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Confirm this year's exact festival dates and full program on the official Tennessee Williams & New Orleans Literary Festival site — the lineup and schedule are announced annually and change year to year
- [ ] Decide which format your group actually wants: ticketed panels and master classes, or the free public events (walking tours, the shouting contest)
- [ ] Book any ticketed sessions early — panels with notable authors and small master classes have limited seating
- [ ] Reserve a spot to watch (or enter) the Stella shouting contest in Jackson Square if that's a group priority — it draws a real crowd
- [ ] Pair the festival with a [French Quarter](/neighborhoods/french-quarter/) walking day, since most of the programming happens in and around the Quarter
- [ ] Treat this as a spring high-season trip for booking purposes — accommodations and restaurants fill on the same clock as Mardi Gras and Jazz Fest

---

## What the Festival Actually Is

**The format.** A week of literary programming: panel discussions with authors and scholars, craft-focused master classes, staged readings and performances, and walking tours that connect New Orleans locations to Tennessee Williams' life and work. It's a literary festival in the conference sense — sessions with start times, not a fairground.

**The venue spread.** Programming happens across multiple French Quarter hotels, theaters, and historic venues rather than one central site. Expect to move between locations over the course of a day, which is a different rhythm than a single-site music festival.

**The shouting contest.** The festival's best-known public event, held in Jackson Square: contestants take turns bellowing Stanley Kowalski's "STELLA!" from *A Streetcar Named Desire*, judged on volume, drama, and commitment to the bit. It's free to watch, draws a genuine crowd, and is the one part of the week built for spectators rather than attendees.

**The audience.** A mix of literary-minded locals, out-of-town writers and readers, and visitors who came for one specific panel or the shouting contest alone. It skews older and more conversational than the city's music festivals — worth knowing if your group is picturing a Jazz Fest crowd.

| Tennessee Williams Festival | Jazz Fest / French Quarter Fest |
|---|---|
| Panels, readings, walking tours | Music stages, food booths |
| Spread across French Quarter venues | Fairgrounds or riverfront footprint |
| Ticketed sessions plus free public events | Mostly ticketed or single-site free |
| Quieter, conversational crowd | Large, high-energy crowd |

## Running It With a Group

### Split by Interest, Not by Itinerary

Not every trip member wants to sit through a panel on Southern Gothic fiction, and that's fine. The festival's spread of ticketed and free programming makes it easy to split the group by interest — some in sessions, others exploring the [French Quarter](/neighborhoods/french-quarter/) on their own — and reconvene for the shared events everyone actually wants, like the shouting contest.

### Build the Day Around Jackson Square

Jackson Square is the natural gathering point for the week, both for the shouting contest and because it sits at the center of the Quarter's other attractions. A group can treat festival days like any other Quarter day: sessions in the morning, food and wandering in the afternoon, the square in the evening.

### Pair It With a Literary Walking Tour

If your group likes the format, extend the literary theme beyond the festival's own walking tours with the [bookshop literary tour guide](/guides/nola-group-bookshop-literary-tour-guide/) — a good add-on for a group that wants a full day leaning into the city's writing history rather than one afternoon of it.

### Treat It Like Any Spring High-Season Trip

Late March sits inside the city's core high-demand stretch. Accommodations, restaurants, and transportation book up on the same schedule as Mardi Gras and French Quarter Fest weekends — plan the trip with that in mind, not as a quiet-week assumption.

## Pro Tips

1. **Check the current year's program before you build sessions into the itinerary.** Author lineups, panel topics, and exact dates are announced annually — don't assume last year's schedule repeats.

2. **Book ticketed panels and master classes early.** Popular sessions with well-known authors have real seating limits, unlike a fairground stage.

3. **Don't skip the shouting contest because it sounds like a gimmick.** It's the one event built for a crowd, and it's genuinely fun to watch even if no one in your group wants to enter.

4. **Mix ticketed and free.** A group doesn't need to buy into every session — one or two panels plus the free public events covers the week well for most trips.

5. **Lean into the French Quarter setting.** Most venues cluster in the Quarter, which makes this an easy festival to combine with a normal Quarter sightseeing day.

6. **Don't expect a music-festival crowd or energy.** This is a conversational, literary week — set expectations with anyone in your group expecting Jazz Fest.

7. **Book your stay like it's spring high season, because it is.** Late March overlaps the city's busiest demand window — don't treat this as an off-peak trip.

---

## For a Big Group

The Tennessee Williams Festival is one of the easier festival weeks to run with a mixed group precisely because it doesn't demand everyone do the same thing at the same time. Panels and walking tours split cleanly by interest, and the shouting contest gives the whole crew a free, shared event to build the evening around.

The trip-level logistics are the same as any other spring week in New Orleans: this sits inside the city's highest-demand stretch, and large-format properties and hotel blocks fill on the same clock as Mardi Gras, French Quarter Fest, and Jazz Fest. For the largest selection, book 6+ months out.
