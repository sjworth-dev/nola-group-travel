Austin and New Orleans both pitch themselves as music towns, which is exactly why group chats keep landing on a side-by-side. Past that headline, they're not close to the same trip.

Austin is built around a lake, a swimming hole, a tech-money food scene, and a live-music identity that's mostly about volume and variety of venues. New Orleans is an older city where jazz, brass, and second-line music aren't a scene — they're the ambient soundtrack of a Tuesday afternoon. Both are legitimate answers for a big group. They are not the same answer.

Here's the honest version, not the one a tourism board would write. (Still working out the basics of the trip itself? The [planning sequence](/guides/plan-a-group-trip/) covers the decisions that come before the city comparison.)

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Decide what your group means by "live music" — a huge volume of bar-band venues, or genre-defining music you can't hear anywhere else
- [ ] Figure out how much your group cares about outdoor day activities — lake time and swimming holes vs. villa-pool culture
- [ ] Check large-group rental inventory in both cities before assuming either is the easier logistics play
- [ ] Price a full weekend per person in both cities, not just the flight
- [ ] Confirm your dates against both cities' event calendars — SXSW and ACL reshape Austin the same way Jazz Fest and Mardi Gras reshape New Orleans
- [ ] Talk about heat honestly. Both cities get brutal in summer, but on different clocks

## Music: Volume vs. Origin

Austin calls itself the Live Music Capital of the World, and the claim holds up on raw venue count — 6th Street alone stacks bars with live bands most nights, and the city supports a huge, genre-spanning scene from country to indie rock to blues. It's a real, deep music town.

New Orleans didn't just host music history, it produced it. Jazz, brass band, R&B, funk, and second-line music were invented here and are still played live, most nights, by musicians carrying on a direct lineage rather than performing a genre. Frenchmen Street is the closest analog to 6th Street, but the music is original, not a cover circuit.

**The honest distinction:** Austin gives your group a huge menu of bars, each with its own band, spread across a scene that's broad but relatively young. New Orleans gives your group music that only exists here, performed by people whose grandparents played the same rooms. If your group wants variety and volume, Austin wins. If your group wants to hear something genuinely singular, New Orleans wins. ## Food: A Real Fight, Different Categories

This one's closer than the Nashville or Charleston comparisons. Austin has built a legitimately excellent food scene — barbecue is the headline, food trucks are a genuine culture rather than a novelty, and Tex-Mex here is taken seriously. It's a strong, modern food city.

New Orleans runs on centuries, not decades. Creole and Cajun cooking developed here as a full culinary tradition long before "food scene" was a phrase anyone used. A group of 20 can eat a different genre of New Orleans food every night for four nights — Creole, Cajun, po'boys, brunch culture, late-night food — without repeating themselves. Check the [food tour vs. self-guided guide](/guides/nola-group-food-tour-vs-self-guided/) for how to organize that for a big crew.

**The honest read:** if your group wants a barbecue-and-tacos trip built around food trucks and patios, Austin delivers that better than almost anywhere. If your group wants food with historical depth and range that carries a whole trip on its own, New Orleans wins.

## Lake Days vs. Villa-Pool Culture

This is the sharpest structural difference between the two cities, and it's worth thinking through before anything else.

Austin's daytime identity runs through the water — Lady Bird Lake for paddleboarding and kayaking, Barton Springs Pool for a swim in cold spring water, a lake culture built around floating, tubing, and being outside during the day. It's a genuinely great setup for a group that wants an active, outdoor afternoon between nights out.

New Orleans doesn't have that natural-water day-trip culture in the same way. Instead, the city's large-group rental market has built its own version of a daytime hangout: villas with private or shared pools designed specifically for a big crew to post up at between activities. It's a manufactured answer to the same problem Austin solves with a literal lake — somewhere for the group to be outside, in water, without leaving base. The [pool day etiquette guide](/guides/nola-group-pool-day-etiquette-guide/) covers how that actually works when the whole crew's involved.

**The honest distinction:** Austin's daytime activity is public and free. New Orleans's is private and built into the villa you rent. Neither is better outright — a group that wants to be out in a public lake scene should lean Austin; a group that wants a private home base to control belongs in New Orleans.

## Villa and Large-Group Rental Landscape

Austin's short-term rental scene for large groups skews toward big suburban houses outside the central corridor, often with a lake view but a rideshare or drive required to reach 6th Street or Rainey Street at night. Purpose-built large-capacity houses close to the action are limited, and Austin's STR regulations inside city limits have tightened in ways that push a lot of big-group inventory further out.

New Orleans has a more developed purpose-built large-group villa market, concentrated in the Bywater and the Lower Garden District. These are properties designed specifically for the whole crew — private or shared pools, full kitchens, a real home base rather than a big house that happens to sleep a lot of people. The [Bywater vs. LGD comparison](/guides/nola-group-bywater-vs-lgd-deep-comparison/) breaks down how those two neighborhoods differ for a group choosing between them.

**Editorial examples of what that inventory looks like:** Castleday Retreats operates villas in the Bywater, each with a private pool. Heirloom's inventory of large-format homes runs through the Lower Garden District and Central City, dozens of properties deep, which matters if you're booking on shorter notice. Neither is a booking recommendation here — they're representative of the kind of purpose-built inventory New Orleans has that Austin's more spread-out, regulation-tightened rental market doesn't match at the same scale.

## Nightlife After Midnight

Austin's bars run on a fairly standard schedule — a great night on 6th Street or Rainey Street still has a closing time, and by 2 AM most of the crowd is heading home or to a late-night taco stand.

New Orleans doesn't have a closing time. Bars can legally stay open all night, and enough of them do that a group can choose to keep going until sunrise or call it early — the option exists either way. That's a structural fact about the city, not an attitude. See the [day drinking vs. nightlife guide](/guides/nola-group-day-drinking-vs-nightlife-guide/) for how to actually pace a group across a full day and night here.

## Heat: Different Problem, Same Season

Both cities get genuinely brutal in summer, but the shape of the problem differs. Austin's summer heat is dry-hot, which the lake and Barton Springs exist partly to solve — jump in, cool off, keep going. New Orleans summer heat comes with heavy humidity and afternoon thunderstorms, which is a different kind of exhausting and less solvable by a quick swim in a lake.

For New Orleans specifically, the smart move for a group that wants to dodge the worst of it is targeting October or the February-through-May stretch — which also happens to be the city's most active season for festivals, not a quiet shoulder window. Check the [festival season calendar](/guides/festival-season-calendar/) before locking dates in either city, since SXSW, ACL, Jazz Fest, and Mardi Gras all reshape their respective cities' pricing and crowds.

## Cost Structure

Austin trips tend to concentrate spend on food trucks, barbecue, and a steady stream of cover charges and drink minimums along 6th Street and Rainey Street. Lake activities add rental costs of their own — tubes, paddleboards, and the shuttle or rideshare to get to the water.

New Orleans spreads cost more evenly across food, music, and villa accommodation, with fewer mandatory tourist-package upsells. The [hotel vs. villa guide](/guides/hotel-vs-villa-guide/) and the [budget guide](/guides/budget-guide/) run the actual per-person math for New Orleans. The short version: a villa with a kitchen and pool for the whole crew often lands at a lower per-person nightly cost than the equivalent hotel block, and it removes the nightly cover-charge tax that a bar-hopping trip usually carries.

## Which Group Should Pick Which City

**Pick Austin if:** your group wants an active, outdoor-heavy trip built around a lake and a swimming hole, a huge volume of live-music bars to bounce between, and genuinely great barbecue and food trucks. It's the higher-energy, sunnier option.

**Pick New Orleans if:** your group cares about music you can't hear anywhere else, wants food with real historical depth, wants a home base with a private pool instead of a rental a rideshare away from downtown, or wants the option of a night that doesn't have to end. It asks a little more of the planning and pays it back.

**Pick New Orleans if your group has already done Austin.** A meaningful share of organizers landing here are repeat bachelorette or milestone-trip planners looking for the next city after Austin or Nashville. If that's your group, this is very likely the better next trip.

## Pro Tips

1. **Don't decide based on Instagram aesthetics alone.** Austin photographs well because the lake and the murals are built for it. New Orleans photographs just as well, but the good spots are less obvious — do a little research before you land.

2. **If cost is the deciding factor, run both budgets fully.** Flights, rental or hotel block, food, and nightlife spend — not just the headline "which city is cheaper" assumption. The gap is smaller than people expect.

3. **Book villa or hotel inventory early in both cities.** Large-group availability is the actual bottleneck in both markets, not price. For the largest selection, book 6+ months out.

4. **Check the festival calendar before locking dates.** New Orleans has a genuinely inverted season — October and February through May are peak, not shoulder. Austin has its own crush weeks around SXSW and ACL. A random-seeming weekend can turn into a very different trip in either city.

5. **If your group has mixed interests, weigh how each city splits.** Austin splits cleanly into "lake people" and "bar people." New Orleans splits into music, food, and villa-pool factions just as easily, with more genre range in the music itself.

6. **Ask what "last night out" means to your group.** If the answer is "we want the option to keep going," that's a New Orleans answer. If the answer is "we want a fun, predictable night that wraps by 2," that's closer to Austin.

7. **Don't assume either city is more walkable than it is.** Research your specific rental's location. An Austin rental outside the downtown corridor and a New Orleans rental outside Bywater or LGD both mean rideshares become part of the daily budget.

## At Full Headcount

The comparison changes shape once a group crosses into double digits. Austin's large-capacity rentals tend to sit outside the walkable core, and tightened short-term rental rules inside city limits push big groups further out still, meaning more rideshare time built into every night.

New Orleans has the more developed version of the purpose-built market — villas built for the whole crew, concentrated in the Bywater and Lower Garden District, of the kind a big group organizing in Austin often has to cobble together from oversized suburban houses instead.

That gap — a villa built for the whole crew versus a house that happens to fit one — is worth weighing as heavily as the music and the lake.
