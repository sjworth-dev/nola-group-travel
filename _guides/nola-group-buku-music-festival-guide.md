Most New Orleans festival guides are written for the Jazz Fest crowd — people who want a fairgrounds, a schedule of seven stages, and a plan for pacing themselves across two long weekends. BUKU is not that festival, and if you bring the Jazz Fest playbook you'll misread it.

BUKU Music + Art Project is a compact, two-day festival on the New Orleans riverfront, generally held in March, with programming that leans electronic, hip-hop, and bass-forward. The crowd skews young. The format is dense: fewer stages, packed close together, running from afternoon into the night. It's a festival built for a group that wants volume and momentum, not a leisurely stroll past a gospel tent.

For a big group, the compactness is the whole advantage. This is the rare festival where twenty people can actually stay loosely together, because the grounds are small enough that "meet at the main stage" is a real instruction and not a fantasy. Here's how to run it.

## Quick Planning Checklist

- [ ] Festival dates and lineup shift every year — confirm the exact March weekend and the day-by-day schedule on the official BUKU site before booking anything
- [ ] Book accommodation 6+ months out for the largest selection of large-group villas

- [ ] Plan for a late-ending night — this festival runs into the night, not a 7 PM fairgrounds close

- [ ] March weather is mild but variable — layers, not shorts-only
- [ ] Line up a plan for after the festival closes; the crew will want to keep going

## What BUKU Actually Is

BUKU is a two-day festival on the river, staged in the warehouse-and-river industrial pocket on the edge of downtown, near Mardi Gras World. The setting is part of the identity: shipping containers, murals, art installations, and stages tucked into a riverfront footprint rather than spread across an open field.

The programming is the other half. Where Jazz Fest and [French Quarter Fest](/guides/french-quarter-fest/) are rooted in New Orleans' own traditions, BUKU pulls from the national and international electronic and hip-hop touring circuit. Expect DJs, producers, rappers, and bass acts alongside a smaller strand of live bands. The lineup is announced months ahead — check it before you commit, because the headliners are the reason a specific group will or won't care about a given year.

The "Art Project" half of the name is real, not decoration. BUKU programs live painting, installations, and roving performers into the grounds. For a group, that gives you something to do between sets that isn't just standing in another crowd.

| Feature | BUKU | Jazz Fest / French Quarter Fest |
|--------|------|--------------------------------|
| Length | Two days | Multiple days / two weekends |
| Genre center | Electronic, hip-hop, bass | New Orleans jazz, brass, roots |
| Crowd age | Younger | Broad, family-inclusive |
| Grounds | Compact, riverfront | Large fairgrounds / spread across the Quarter |
| Daily rhythm | Afternoon into the night | Midday into early evening |
| Group cohesion | Easy — small footprint | Hard — spread out |

If your group's median age is closer to Jazz Fest's, and the point of the trip is New Orleans' own music, BUKU is the wrong anchor — the [festival season calendar](/guides/festival-season-calendar/) lays out the alternatives by month. If the crew wants a modern festival experience that happens to be in New Orleans, BUKU is exactly right.

## The Case for the Compact Festival

The single biggest problem with running a large group at a festival is that everyone drifts, and by 4 PM you have twenty people scattered across a square mile with dying phones. BUKU mostly solves this by being small.

You can walk the grounds end to end in a few minutes. Stages are close enough that you can hear one act while walking to another. That changes the group math entirely:

- **A rally point actually works.** Pick a landmark — a specific stage, an art installation, a food area — and "back here at 8" is a plan people can keep.
- **Splitting up is low-risk.** Half the crew chases one act, half chases another, and reconvening costs five minutes, not an hour.
- **Nobody gets truly lost.** The footprint is small enough that a wandering member finds their way back without a search party.

This is the opposite of a sprawling fest, where the [festival exit strategy](/guides/nola-group-festival-exit-strategy-guide/) has to account for a long, staggered walk to scattered rideshare points. At BUKU, the exit is short — which matters, because the crowd all leaves at once when the headliner ends.

## Where to Base the Group

BUKU's riverfront location near Mardi Gras World sits at the edge of the [Lower Garden District](/neighborhoods/lower-garden-district/) and the river side of downtown. That makes basing decisions easy: you want to be close enough to walk or take a very short ride home at the end of a late night.

**Lower Garden District** is the natural home base. It's the closest residential-scale neighborhood to the grounds, it's walkable, and it's stocked with the kind of large-format properties a group needs. A short walk home after a festival that ends late is worth more than most people realize when they're booking in the abstract — at midnight, a ten-minute walk beats a forty-minute rideshare surge every time.

**Warehouse District / CBD** works too, and puts you near the grounds on the other side, with restaurants and bars within reach for the pre-game and the after.

**Bywater or Marigny** is livable but adds a rideshare each way. If the group's priority is a specific villa over proximity, it's a fine trade — just build the transport time into the plan and expect surge pricing at close.

Wherever you land, the [festival pre-game guide](/guides/nola-group-festival-pre-game-guide/) covers how to feed and stage a big group before the gates so you're not trying to organize twenty people who are already three drinks in.

## Running the Two Days

### Day One: Read the Crowd

Arrive after the gates open but before the headliner window. The first day is when the group calibrates — how hard everyone's going, who's pacing for two days and who's not, where the good spots are.

Set the rally point on the way in. Do a full loop of the grounds early, before it fills, so everyone has a mental map. Catch a mid-afternoon set together, then let the group fracture into interest groups for the evening.

The headliner sets run late. This is not a festival where you're back at the house by nine. Plan the night around a late close and a short walk home.

### Day Two: Conserve and Commit

Day two of a two-day festival is where groups fall apart if they went too hard on day one. The move is a slow morning — long brunch, no alarm, hydrate — and a later, more deliberate arrival.

By day two the group knows the grounds and knows the acts they actually want. Skip the drift. Pick the three or four sets that matter and build the day around them. The [festival exit strategy guide](/guides/nola-group-festival-exit-strategy-guide/) matters more on the final night, when everyone leaves at once and the whole crowd wants a ride simultaneously — a short walk home is the cleanest exit there is.

## What to Pack and Wear

March in New Orleans is mild but genuinely variable. It can be a warm afternoon and a cold night in the same day, and the riverfront gets a breeze off the water after dark. This is not August — the problem isn't heat, it's a temperature swing.

- **Layers.** A daytime layer and a night layer. The riverfront gets cool once the sun's down.
- **Real shoes.** The grounds are industrial riverfront — pavement, gravel, and packed dirt, not a soft field.
- **A charged phone and a plan for when it isn't.** Set the rally point and a reconnect time before you lose signal in the crowd.
- **Check the bag policy first.** Festival bag rules change and are strict; confirm sizes on the official site before packing.

The [music festival packing guide](/guides/nola-music-festival-packing-guide/) has the full big-group packing breakdown; the short version for BUKU is: dress for a temperature swing, not a single forecast.

## Pro Tips

1. **Buy the two-day pass as a group, early.** Single-day tickets seem cheaper until they split the crew across different days. If the whole point is doing this together, everyone needs the same pass. Prices climb as the festival approaches.

2. **Do the full-grounds loop before you commit to a spot.** The layout is compact but dense. Ten minutes walking the whole footprint on day one saves you from planting at a mediocre stage while the good one is a two-minute walk away.

3. **Set the rally point at a fixed landmark, not a stage.** Stages change acts and crowds; a specific art installation or a food area stays put. "Meet at the mural" beats "meet at the stage where that DJ was."

4. **Eat before the gates, not inside.** The sets don't pause for dinner and the good acts stack up at night. Feed the group first — the [pre-game guide](/guides/nola-group-festival-pre-game-guide/) covers staging a big crew before a festival — so nobody's peeling off to hunt down food during the set they came for.

5. **Base within walking distance if you possibly can.** The single best BUKU decision is a short walk home. The whole crowd leaves at once when the headliner ends, rideshare surges hard, and a group of twenty splitting into rideshares at midnight is chaos. Walking home is the cheat code.

6. **Pace day one for day two.** It's a two-day festival, not a one-night blowout. The groups that treat day one like the only day are useless by day two's headliner. Bank some energy.

7. **Check the age and gate policies before you build the group.** BUKU skews young and has specific entry rules. Confirm them early so nobody in the crew hits a surprise at the gate.

BUKU's riverfront location is its own accommodation brief: you want a large-format property close enough to walk home from a festival that ends late. That's a different priority than a fairgrounds festival, where proximity matters less because everyone's leaving mid-evening and spread out anyway.

The [Lower Garden District](/neighborhoods/lower-garden-district/) is the strongest fit, because it combines walkable proximity to the grounds with the kind of whole-house inventory a big group needs. If the group prioritizes a specific villa over the short walk, Castleday Retreats' three Bywater villas each sleep 14–30 guests with private pools — a longer ride to the grounds, but total privacy for the base. The trade is walkability versus seclusion; for a late-ending festival, most groups should weight walkability.

## The Verdict

BUKU is the right festival for a specific group: younger, up for a modern electronic-and-hip-hop bill, and more interested in a dense two-day format than a sprawling celebration of New Orleans' own traditions. If that's the crew, the compact riverfront grounds make it one of the easiest large festivals in the city to run as a group — the whole thing is small enough to actually stay together.

If the group's center of gravity is jazz, brass, and the city's own sound, this is the wrong anchor and the [live music guide](/guides/live-music-guide/) points you at the right nights instead. But for the crew that wants a real festival experience with the New Orleans river at their back and a short walk home, BUKU delivers — book close, buy the two-day pass, and pace it for two days, not one.
