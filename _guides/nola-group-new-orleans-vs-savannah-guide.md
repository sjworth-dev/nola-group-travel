**The short answer:** pick Savannah for a small, pretty, contained weekend that ends at a reasonable hour; pick New Orleans for live music, food with real depth, a night with no mandatory ending, and — decisively — if your group is large, because Savannah's regulated downtown rental market makes housing a big crew together genuinely hard. The rest of this page earns that verdict category by category.

Savannah and New Orleans land on the same shortlist because they share a rare trait: both are genuinely walkable historic Southern cities where you can legally carry a drink down the sidewalk. For a bachelorette or a milestone girls' trip, that combination — old architecture, Spanish moss or wrought iron, and an open-container culture — narrows the field fast.

But they are not the same trip. Savannah is small, gorgeous, and self-contained — a compact grid of oak-shaded squares you can cross on foot in twenty minutes. New Orleans is bigger, louder, older as a cultural force, and has no closing time. Both are legitimate. They suit different crews.

Here's the version without the visitors-bureau gloss.

## The Open-Container Thing: Both Cities, With a Catch

This is the feature that puts both cities on the same list, so start here. Both let you walk around with a drink — but the rules differ, and the difference matters for a group.

Savannah's Historic District allows one drink to-go, capped at 16 ounces, in a plastic cup, within a defined downtown boundary. Step outside that boundary and the rule ends. It's a real perk, and it's genuinely fun, but it's a permission with edges.

New Orleans is looser: a plastic go-cup is a citywide norm, not a bounded downtown exception, and there's no closing time forcing the party to a stop. The mechanics of where it applies and where it doesn't are worth knowing before a bar crawl — the [open container law guide](/guides/nola-group-open-container-law-guide/) breaks down exactly where a go-cup is fine and where it isn't.

**The honest read:** Savannah gives your group a charming, bounded version of the street-drink experience. New Orleans gives your group the full, unbounded version. If that specific feature is why the trip is happening, New Orleans is the deeper cut.

## Nightlife: A Structural Difference, Not a Vibe Difference

Savannah's nightlife centers on City Market and River Street — a walkable cluster of bars, a few live-music rooms, and a rooftop or two. It's fun and easy to work as a group because everything is close together. But Savannah runs on a conventional clock: bars have last call, and the night has a natural end whether the group is ready or not.

New Orleans doesn't have a closing time. Bars can legally stay open all night, and enough do that a group genuinely chooses when the night ends — keep going until sunrise or wrap early, the option is always there. That's a structural fact about the city, not an attitude. See the [day drinking vs. nightlife guide](/guides/nola-group-day-drinking-vs-nightlife-guide/) for how a big crew actually paces a full day into a full night here.

**The distinction:** Savannah hands your group a clean, contained night in a small, pretty footprint. New Orleans hands your group the option to not contain it. Which is better depends entirely on what your crew means by "a good last night."

## Music: Not a Close Comparison

Savannah has a real bar scene and a respectable local music circuit, and it hosts a well-regarded music festival in spring. But live music is not the organizing principle of a night out there the way it is in New Orleans.

New Orleans didn't just host music history — it produced it. Jazz, brass band, R&B, funk, and second-line music aren't a nostalgia act here; they're what's playing live, most nights, in rooms that have been doing it for generations. Frenchmen Street runs more original live music on a Tuesday than most cities manage on a weekend. If your group wants music as the backbone of the trip rather than background to it, that's a decisive gap — the [live music guide](/guides/live-music-guide/) and the [jazz clubs vs. music bars guide](/guides/nola-group-jazz-clubs-vs-music-bars-guide/) show what a NOLA music night actually looks like.

## Food: Both Good — But Different Weight Classes

Savannah eats well. It shares the Lowcountry and Southern tradition that makes coastal Georgia and South Carolina genuinely good food regions — shrimp and grits, a strong seafood lineage, a handful of destination restaurants that draw a line out the door.

New Orleans is one of the best food cities in the country, full stop. Creole and Cajun cooking developed here over centuries as a complete culinary tradition, not a recent renaissance. A big group can eat an entirely different genre every night — Creole, Cajun, po'boys, brunch culture, late-night food — for four nights without repeating itself. The [food tour vs. self-guided guide](/guides/nola-group-food-tour-vs-self-guided/) covers how to organize that for a crowd.

**The read:** if food is a highlight of the trip rather than fuel between bars, New Orleans wins comfortably. Savannah is good; it's not the same depth.

## Villa and Large-Group Rental Landscape

This is where the two cities diverge most, and it's usually the last thing a group researches.

Savannah's Historic District regulates and caps short-term rentals tightly. The city limits the number of vacation-rental permits downtown, which pushes large-group inventory either toward smaller licensed units or out to the edges. Housing a big crew together inside the walkable historic core — the whole reason people pick Savannah — is genuinely hard, and often means splitting across several rentals.

New Orleans has a more developed purpose-built large-group market, concentrated in the Bywater and the Lower Garden District. These are properties designed for double-digit groups — private or shared pools, full kitchens, a real home base rather than a family house that happens to sleep a lot of people. The [Bywater vs. LGD comparison](/guides/nola-group-bywater-vs-lgd-deep-comparison/) breaks down how those two neighborhoods differ for a group choosing between them.

**What that inventory actually looks like** (plain examples, not recommendations): purpose-built villas like Castleday Retreats in the Bywater or The Syd in the Lower Garden District house a big crew under one roof with a pool. Deep-inventory operators like Heirloom carry dozens of large-format homes for short-notice choice. Hotel Perle or The Revelry offer group-suite and apartment-floor setups when separate bills matter. And the classic hotels — The Roosevelt, Hotel Peter & Paul, The Pontchartrain — run room blocks for mixed-budget crews. Savannah's regulated core doesn't have an equivalent depth of purpose-built, walkable, whole-crew inventory. The full breakdown lives on the [where to stay page](/where-to-stay/).

## Walkability: Savannah's Real Edge

Give Savannah its due. Its historic core is one of the most walkable in the country — a tidy grid of squares where a group can drift from brunch to a square to a bar without a single rideshare. For a crew that wants everything within a fifteen-minute stroll, Savannah's footprint is smaller and tighter than New Orleans's.

New Orleans is walkable in stretches — the French Quarter, Marigny, and the Frenchmen corridor connect on foot — but a full NOLA trip spreads across neighborhoods, and rideshares become part of the daily rhythm. That's a genuine point for Savannah if compactness is the priority. The trade-off is that Savannah's small footprint is also its ceiling: you can see most of it quickly, where New Orleans keeps unfolding. Sort out how a group actually moves here with the [transportation guide](/guides/transportation-guide/) and the [ride-share master guide](/guides/nola-group-ride-share-master-guide/).

## Heat and Humidity: Basically a Wash

Both cities sit in the same coastal-Southern climate band, and neither is a cool-weather destination for most of the year. Summers in both are hot, humid, and prone to afternoon storms.

For New Orleans specifically, a group trying to dodge the worst heat should target October or the February-through-May stretch — which is also the city's liveliest, highest-demand season, not a quiet shoulder window. Don't assume one city solves the heat and the other doesn't; compare specific date ranges instead. If you want the genuine value window, it's late August into mid-September, before October's demand ramps.

## Cost Structure

Savannah trips concentrate spend on the constrained downtown rental market — scarce, walkable, licensed inventory commands a premium — plus dining and the bar cluster. New Orleans spreads spend more evenly across food, music, and accommodation, and a private villa with a kitchen changes the math for a big group.

The [hotel vs. villa guide](/guides/hotel-vs-villa-guide/) and the [budget guide](/guides/budget-guide/) run the actual per-person numbers for New Orleans. The short version: a villa with a pool and kitchen for a big crew often lands at a lower per-person nightly cost than the equivalent hotel block, and it cuts down on eating every meal out.

## Savannah vs New Orleans: Which Should Your Group Pick?

**Pick Savannah if:** your group wants a small, elegant, walkable trip — a pretty historic grid you can cross on foot, a to-go drink in the Historic District, good Lowcountry food, and a night that ends at a reasonable hour. It asks very little of you logistically once you're downtown.

**Pick New Orleans if:** your group wants music as the backbone of the trip, wants the option of a night that doesn't have to end, cares about food with real range and history, wants the unbounded version of the street-drink culture, or wants a home base with a pool built for the whole crew instead of several licensed rentals stitched together.

**Pick New Orleans if the group is large.** Savannah's regulated downtown rental market makes housing a big crew together in the walkable core genuinely hard. That alone pushes a lot of larger groups toward New Orleans, independent of vibe.

## For a Bachelorette Party Specifically

This is the matchup where these two cities most often go head-to-head, so here's the direct verdict. Savannah is the gentler bachelorette: pretty backdrops, a to-go cup in the squares, wine on a rooftop, everyone in bed by one. If the bride's ideal weekend is charming rather than chaotic, Savannah delivers it with less planning. New Orleans is the fuller version of the same idea — the street drinks don't stop at a district line, the music is live instead of piped, the night ends when the group decides, and a double-digit party can actually sleep under one roof. Most bachelorette crews past ten people end up in New Orleans for the housing math alone; the [bachelorette party guide](/guides/bachelorette-party/) has the full plan if that's where this lands.

## Pro Tips

1. **Don't decide off photos alone.** Savannah photographs effortlessly — the squares are built for it. New Orleans photographs just as well, but the best spots take a little research to find.

2. **Run the rental math for your actual headcount.** A crew of ten has options in both cities. A crew past twenty will find New Orleans's villa market far deeper than Savannah's capped downtown inventory.

3. **Book rental inventory early in both.** Availability, not price, is the real bottleneck for a big group. For the largest selection in New Orleans, book 6+ months out.

4. **Learn each city's open-container boundary before the crawl.** Savannah's is a bounded 16-ounce, plastic-cup rule inside the Historic District. New Orleans's is looser but still has rules worth knowing — glass, for one, is a problem.

5. **Check the New Orleans festival calendar before locking dates.** The season is inverted from most destinations — October and February through May are peak, not shoulder — so a random-looking weekend can be a major event weekend.

6. **Ask what "a good last night" means to your group.** "We want it to wind down naturally" is a Savannah answer. "We want the option to keep going" is a New Orleans answer.

7. **Don't assume walkability is a wash.** Savannah's core is tighter than New Orleans's. If everything-within-a-stroll is the top priority, weigh that honestly — then weigh it against everything Savannah's smaller footprint can't hold.

---

## For a Big Group Specifically

The comparison shifts hard once a crew goes double-digit. Savannah's Historic District rental caps mean purpose-built, large-capacity, walkable inventory is scarce, which pushes big groups toward compromises on location or splitting across several licensed rentals.

New Orleans has the more developed version of that market — from single-villa properties built for a whole crew, to deep large-format home inventory, to hotel room blocks for mixed budgets and separate bills. That gap — one place built for the entire group versus several smaller rentals cobbled together — is worth weighing as heavily as the food and the music.
