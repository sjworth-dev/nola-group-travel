Somebody's getting married, somebody just had a kid, somebody moved back in with their parents at 34 and doesn't want to talk about it. Ten years ago you were the same. Now you're not, and the trip has to work anyway.

A college reunion trip is not a bachelorette party and it's not a guys' golf weekend. There's no single occasion pulling everyone toward the same activities. The actual goal — the one nobody says out loud in the group chat — is finding out if the friendship still works in person, not just in a thread with 400 unread messages. That takes unstructured time in a way most trip formats don't.

New Orleans is unusually well suited to that. It's a city you can do nothing in and still feel like you did something — a porch, a pool, a po'boy, a jazz set nobody planned to see. That matters more here than the itinerary does.

## Why This Trip Is Different

A bachelorette party has a guest of honor and an obvious center of gravity. A guys' weekend has golf tee times and a game to watch. A college reunion has none of that. Everyone showed up for the same reason — each other — which sounds simple until you're actually planning around it.

Three things make this trip type harder than it looks:

**The life-stage gap.** Someone's on a baby's nap schedule via FaceTime. Someone just got a signing bonus and wants to spend it. Someone's between jobs and doing math on every menu. All four of you were broke college students together. Now you're not the same broke, or not broke at all, and the trip has to flex for that without anyone feeling singled out.

**The catch-up-vs-activity tension.** Book too much and nobody actually talks — you're just four people standing in the same tour group. Book too little and the trip feels aimless, especially for the friend who flew farthest and wants it to feel like an event. The fix is picking two or three real plans and leaving the rest of the trip open.

**The "who's changed" factor.** You're not just catching up on news. You're recalibrating who this person is now — new partner, new politics, new relationship with drinking, new everything. That takes real conversation time, which mostly happens in kitchens and on porches, not at a table for twelve at a loud restaurant.

None of this is a New Orleans problem specifically, but New Orleans handles it better than most destination options. There's no single "must-do" that swallows the trip the way a beach or a ski slope does, so the group isn't forced into one activity that only half of them actually want.

## Structuring the Time: Anchor Plans, Not a Full Itinerary

Resist the urge to fill every block. A reunion trip runs better on two or three fixed plans surrounded by open time than on a tight day-by-day schedule.

| Time Block | What Works | What to Skip |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival evening | Groceries, drinks at the house, low-key catching up | A big reservation on night one — people are tired and travel-scattered |
| One full day | A single anchor activity everyone opts into (food tour, swamp tour, museum) | Back-to-back activities with no gap between them |
| Most afternoons | Open time — pool, porch, wandering a neighborhood in twos and threes | A packed schedule that never lets the group split up |
| At least one dinner | One reservation everyone commits to, ideally with a private-ish table | Multiple big reservations on consecutive nights — budget and energy both run out |
| At least one night | No plan. Cook at the house, sit outside, talk | Forcing a night out because "we're only here once" |

The point of the open blocks isn't laziness — it's where the actual reunion happens. The scheduled activities give the trip shape; the unscheduled hours are why everyone came.

## The Mixed-Budget Problem

This is the single biggest source of reunion-trip friction, and it's specific to this trip type in a way it isn't for, say, a bachelorette party where spend expectations are usually set going in.

**Solve it before you land:**

1. Name a rough per-day spend range in the group chat early — "figure $60-80/day outside the house" — so nobody's silently doing math at the table
2. Split the big fixed costs (the house, groceries) evenly, since everyone benefits equally regardless of income
3. Let optional extras — a spa add-on, a tour, a nicer bottle of wine — be opt-in and separately paid, not folded into the group total
4. Pick one "everyone's in" dinner and keep the rest of the food budget flexible so people can eat at their own comfort level
5. Use the [mid-trip budget tracking guide](/guides/nola-group-budget-tracking-mid-trip-guide/) if spending starts drifting by day two — better to recalibrate than to let it go unspoken

The goal isn't making everyone spend the same amount. It's making sure nobody has to either overspend to keep up or quietly opt out of half the trip.

## Neighborhood and Pace

For a reunion crew, the neighborhood matters less than having one good home base everyone can return to between activities. The [Bywater vs. Lower Garden District comparison](/guides/nola-group-bywater-vs-lgd-deep-comparison/) breaks down the tradeoff in more depth, but the short version for this trip type:

- **Bywater** suits a crew that wants a quieter, more residential feel with an easy walk to Frenchmen Street when people are ready for music
- **Lower Garden District** suits a crew that wants streetcar access and easy movement around the city without relying on rideshares for everything

Either works. What matters more is booking one house big enough that the group isn't split across two rentals — splitting the group defeats the entire point of a reunion trip.

## Large Group Section: One House, Real Common Space

The accommodation choice matters more for a reunion trip than almost any other trip type on this site. The whole point is being in the same room, which a scattered set of hotel rooms actively works against.

Look for a house with a genuine common area — a big kitchen, a porch, a pool deck — not just a collection of bedrooms. That common space is where the trip actually happens. As editorial examples of what New Orleans's large-group rental market looks like: Castleday Retreats runs three villas in the Bywater, each holding up to 30 guests with a private pool, built for groups that want total privacy. The Syd runs multiple villas in the Lower Garden District, each up to 22 guests, with a shared pool and hot tub and a more social layout. Neither is the only option — see the [full accommodation guide](/where-to-stay/) for the complete field, including hotels for mixed-budget groups who'd rather book and pay separately.

A reunion crew with wide budget variance is also a case where hotel room blocks are worth a real look instead of defaulting to a villa — separate bills mean nobody's subsidizing anybody else's room, even if it costs the group its shared common space.

## Pro Tips

1. **Don't recreate college.** Nobody wants to relive the version of themselves from a decade ago for three days straight. Let the trip be what this group is now.

2. **Build in a buffer night.** Arrival day and departure day both run long. Don't schedule anything major on either.

3. **Assign one point person.** Reunions have no natural organizer the way a bachelorette has a maid of honor. Someone has to volunteer, or logistics drift. The [trip roles guide](/guides/nola-group-trip-roles-assignment-guide/) breaks down how to split that load beyond just one person.

4. **Ask about drinking comfort directly.** More reunion crews than you'd expect include someone who's cut back or stopped entirely since college. Don't assume the drinking pace from ten years ago still applies.

5. **Keep one dinner reservation flexible on size.** Reunion RSVPs shift more than other trip types — someone always adds a plus-one or drops late. Book a restaurant that can flex by two or three seats.

6. **Take the group photo early.** By the last night, everyone's tired and half the group has left. Get the whole-crew photo on day one or two.

7. **Plan a follow-up before you leave.** The energy to "definitely do this again" is highest on the last day. Pick a rough next date while you're all still in the same city.
